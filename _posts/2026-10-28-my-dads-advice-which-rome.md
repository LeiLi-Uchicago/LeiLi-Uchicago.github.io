𝗠𝘆 𝗗𝗮𝗱'𝘀 𝗔𝗱𝘃𝗶𝗰𝗲 𝗪𝗮𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗪𝗿𝗼𝗻𝗴. 𝗜𝘁 𝗖𝗮𝗺𝗲 𝗙𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝗮 𝗗𝗶𝗳𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗪𝗼𝗿𝗹𝗱.

When I visited my parents recently, my dad gave me some career advice. It was thoughtful, practical, and probably worked well in the system where he built his career.

But his world and mine are very different.

That makes his advice easy for me to interpret. I know where it came from, so I can adjust for the context.

"Good advice, for his world."

Human experience is often narrow, but 𝗶𝘁 𝘂𝘀𝘂𝗮𝗹𝗹𝘆 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗲𝘀 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘁𝗲𝘅𝘁. We know where it was learned, under what rules, and in what kind of environment it worked.

AI is almost the opposite. It has learned from many countries, industries, generations, and cultures. That breadth is powerful, but 𝘁𝗵𝗼𝘀𝗲 𝗲𝘅𝗽𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲𝘀 𝗼𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗻 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝗳𝗹𝗶𝗰𝘁:

Be assertive. Respect hierarchy.

Move fast. Be patient.

Promote yourself. Let your work speak for itself.

𝗔𝗹𝗹 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘀𝗲 𝗰𝗮𝗻 𝗯𝗲 𝗴𝗼𝗼𝗱 𝗮𝗱𝘃𝗶𝗰𝗲, 𝗷𝘂𝘀𝘁 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗶𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗮𝗺𝗲 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗹𝗱.

When context is missing, AI may mix several worlds together, or 𝗾𝘂𝗶𝗲𝘁𝗹𝘆 𝗮𝘀𝘀𝘂𝗺𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗼𝘀𝘁 𝗳𝗮𝗺𝗶𝗹𝗶𝗮𝗿 𝗼𝗻𝗲. The second case is more dangerous, because the answer can sound perfectly reasonable.

Imagine you time-travel and wake up as a soldier. You ask a local, "Is this the Roman Empire?"

"Yes!"

Great. You grab your sword, shout "For the glory of Rome!", and step outside the walls.

It's Rome. But it's also 1453. You're in Constantinople, and the city is about to fall. Good luck, mate.

Nobody lied to you. The Byzantines called themselves Romans. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗹𝗮𝗯𝗲𝗹 𝘄𝗮𝘀 𝗰𝗼𝗿𝗿𝗲𝗰𝘁. You just filled in the missing context and picked the most familiar "Rome."

AI does this more often than we think.

The problem isn't that AI lacks experience.

𝗜𝘁'𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗶𝘁𝘀 𝗲𝘅𝗽𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲 𝗼𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗻 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗲𝘀 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝗮𝗻 𝗮𝗱𝗱𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘀.

With my dad, I never need to ask. With AI, I do:

Which world are we talking about?

Or more simply:

𝗪𝗵𝗶𝗰𝗵 𝗥𝗼𝗺𝗲?
